000		Weakly supervised Dixon-derived representation learning for multiclass L3 skeletal muscle segmentation in abdominal MRI
001		Abstract
002		Automated skeletal muscle assessment at the third lumbar vertebra (L3) is widely used in body-composition analysis. However, most established L3 segmentation pipelines have been developed for computed tomography, leaving magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-only cohorts with fewer validated tools for skeletal muscle index (SMI) analysis. This gap is particularly relevant when compartment-level measurements of abdominal wall, psoas, and paraspinal muscles are required. We developed a weakly supervised Dixon-derived representation learning framework for multiclass L3 skeletal muscle segmentation in abdominal MRI. The model uses in-phase and opposed-phase Dixon images as raw inputs and derives two auxiliary maps from their signal relationship: an absolute difference map and a normalized contrast map. These maps supervise intermediate representation channels, which are combined with the original Dixon inputs for final segmentation. The framework was evaluated in 150 internal cases with five-fold model development and in 55 independent external test cases. It was compared with direct input strategies, alternative backbones, official baseline networks, and ablated variants. Across the three muscle classes, the proposed model achieved a mean foreground Dice of 0.9099, intersection over union of 0.8397, 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance of 4.0757, and average symmetric surface distance of 1.2785. Predicted-mask-derived SMI showed strong agreement with manual-mask-derived SMI for total and abdominal wall muscle. Manual-mask-derived total, abdominal wall, and psoas SMI were also associated with malnutrition after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI. These findings support Dixon MRI-based L3 muscle segmentation as a quantitative tool for body-composition analysis in MRI-only patient cohorts.

006		Introduction
007		Quantitative assessment of skeletal muscle at the third lumbar vertebra (L3) is widely used in body-composition analysis. Cross-sectional muscle area at this level can be normalized by height to derive the skeletal muscle index (SMI). This index has been used to characterize low muscle mass, sarcopenia-related phenotypes, and adverse clinical status in cancer and chronic disease (Mourtzakis et al. 2008; Prado et al. 2008; Derstine et al. 2021). Although total muscle area is commonly reported, the L3 region contains distinct anatomical compartments, including abdominal wall, psoas, and paraspinal muscles. Compartment-level measurement may therefore provide a more detailed description of regional muscle status than a single total muscle index.
008		Most automated L3 body-composition pipelines have been developed for computed tomography (CT). CT provides standardized Hounsfield-unit information and relatively consistent muscle-to-fat contrast. These properties have supported threshold-based measurements and large-scale deep learning systems for abdominal body-composition analysis (Weston et al. 2019; Ha et al. 2021). As a result, CT-based L3 skeletal muscle segmentation is comparatively mature, with published systems addressing L3 slice selection, muscle and adipose tissue segmentation, and large-cohort quantification. However, many patients undergoing abdominal imaging have magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) rather than suitable CT data. In these MRI-only cohorts, the lack of mature automated L3 muscle segmentation tools limits SMI-based body-composition and nutrition-related analyses.
009		MRI is attractive for body-composition assessment because it avoids ionizing radiation and provides strong soft-tissue contrast. Dixon MRI is especially relevant because in-phase and opposed-phase acquisitions encode signal differences related to water–fat composition and tissue interfaces. Prior MRI body-composition studies have shown the feasibility of automated adipose tissue and muscle-related measurements, especially in abdominal or whole-body Dixon MRI settings (Borga 2018; Huber et al. 2020; Estrada et al. 2019; Küstner et al. 2020; Nowak et al. 2025). Nevertheless, MRI-based L3 muscle analysis remains less developed than CT-based analysis. This gap is particularly clear for multiclass segmentation of abdominal wall, psoas, and paraspinal muscles. Technically, MRI intensity values are less standardized than CT Hounsfield units, and muscle boundaries can be affected by sequence variability, motion artifacts, partial-volume effects, and fat infiltration. Comparative work on CT and MRI abdominal muscle quantification has also emphasized the need for careful boundary definition and image-specific handling (Park et al. 2019).
010		These challenges suggest that CT-oriented segmentation strategies may not transfer directly to Dixon MRI. They also suggest that simply adding handcrafted image-derived channels may not fully exploit Dixon-specific information. The relationship between in-phase and opposed-phase images contains tissue-relevant contrast that can emphasize muscle–fat interfaces and relative signal changes. We therefore construct two Dixon-derived maps: an absolute signal-difference map, , and a normalized contrast map, . Rather than feeding these maps directly into the segmentation backbone, we use them as weak supervision targets for an intermediate representation branch. The model receives only in-phase and opposed-phase images as raw inputs, learns Dixon-guided representations, and combines these representations with the raw inputs for L3 multiclass muscle segmentation.
011		In this study, we propose a weakly supervised Dixon-derived representation learning framework for automated L3 skeletal muscle segmentation from abdominal MRI. We evaluate the method in a five-fold setting using mean foreground Dice, intersection over union, Hausdorff distance, and average symmetric surface distance. We compare it with direct input strategies, alternative backbones, official baseline networks, and ablated variants of the representation design. Beyond segmentation accuracy, we assess whether predicted masks can reproduce manual-mask-derived SMI for total skeletal muscle and the three anatomical compartments. This design links the technical segmentation task to MRI-derived body-composition analysis in patients for whom CT-based L3 assessment may not be available.
012		Related work
013		CT-based L3 body-composition analysis
014		Automated body-composition analysis at L3 has been studied most extensively on CT. Early quantitative studies established routine CT as a source for body-composition measurement and supported height-normalized L3 muscle area as an imaging-derived muscle index (Mourtzakis et al. 2008; Prado et al. 2008). Subsequent deep learning systems extended this workflow by segmenting abdominal muscle and adipose compartments and by automating L3 slice selection in large CT cohorts (Weston et al. 2019; Ha et al. 2021). These systems are directly relevant to the clinical motivation of this study. However, their assumptions are CT-specific because tissue appearance is tied to Hounsfield-unit information. CT image contrast also differs substantially from MRI. Therefore, CT-based pipelines do not remove the need for MRI-specific L3 muscle segmentation tools in MRI-only cohorts.
015		MRI and Dixon-based body-composition analysis
016		MRI-based body-composition analysis has developed along related but distinct lines. Prior work has summarized automated MRI analysis of adipose tissue and muscle composition. Dixon MRI has also supported abdominal adipose tissue segmentation and whole-body or large-cohort body-composition quantification (Borga 2018; Huber et al. 2020; Estrada et al. 2019; Küstner et al. 2020; Nowak et al. 2025). Recent L3 MRI tools further indicate that single-slice MRI body-composition analysis is technically feasible (Akella et al. 2025). However, much of this work focuses on adipose tissue compartments, total skeletal muscle, whole-body volumetric measurements, or generic body-composition outputs. The present study addresses a narrower but clinically useful task. It performs multiclass L3 segmentation of abdominal wall, psoas, and paraspinal muscles, followed by total and compartment-specific SMI estimation from abdominal MRI.
017		Deep learning backbones for medical image segmentation
018		Encoder–decoder convolutional networks have become standard tools for biomedical image segmentation. U-Net introduced a symmetric encoder–decoder design with skip connections for precise localization (Ronneberger et al. 2015). Later variants modified this design through nested skip pathways, attention gates, residual learning, or atrous spatial context modeling (Zhou et al. 2020; Oktay et al. 2018; He et al. 2016; Chen et al. 2018). These architectural changes can improve feature propagation, contextual aggregation, or region focusing. They therefore provide natural baselines for MRI muscle segmentation. Nevertheless, backbone design alone does not determine how Dixon-specific in-phase and opposed-phase signal relationships should be incorporated. This distinction motivates our separate evaluation of backbone replacement and Dixon-derived representation learning.
019		Auxiliary representation learning for segmentation
020		Auxiliary supervision and intermediate representation learning provide another route for improving segmentation models. In medical image segmentation, deep supervision has been used to guide intermediate network layers and improve optimization (Dou et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2019). Related computer-vision work has used side-output supervision to encourage meaningful intermediate predictions (Xie and Tu 2015). In contrast to these generic auxiliary losses, the proposed approach uses image-derived Dixon maps as weak supervision targets for learned representation channels. The segmentation masks remain fully supervised. The intermediate representation, however, is guided by two tissue-relevant quantities derived from the in-phase and opposed-phase images. This design differs from direct multichannel input. The handcrafted maps are not fed to the segmentation backbone. Instead, the network learns Dixon-guided representations that are combined with the raw Dixon inputs for segmentation.
021		Methods
022		This retrospective study developed and evaluated a Dixon MRI-based framework for multiclass L3 skeletal muscle segmentation and SMI estimation. The methodological workflow is summarized in Fig. 1. The model segmented three anatomically defined muscle compartments at the L3 level: abdominal wall muscle, psoas muscle, and paraspinal muscle.

027		Study design, cohort, and eligibility criteria
028		The study was conducted as a retrospective, single-center analysis of abdominal MRI examinations from an anonymized institution and department. The internal dataset included 150 cases for model development and internal evaluation. An additional 55 cases were used as an independent external test cohort. These external cases were not used during training, validation, or model selection. Clinical SMI analysis used a subset of 128 cases with complete height information and nutrition-status labels.
029		Cases were excluded if any of the following conditions were present: missing T1 Dixon in-phase or opposed-phase images; incomplete MRI coverage of the L3 level or inability to identify the L3 center slice; missing nutrition-status label; missing height information required for SMI calculation; or severe image artifacts that prevented reliable annotation of L3 skeletal muscle boundaries. Nutrition status was assigned by the nutrition department. Cases labeled as malnutrition () were selected from patients clinically determined to have malnutrition. Cases labeled as normal nutritional status () were determined by the same department as nutritionally normal.
030		MRI acquisition
031		MRI examinations were acquired using a Siemens 1.5-T scanner for abdominal imaging. In-phase and opposed-phase images were obtained from a transverse breath-hold T1-weighted 2D gradient-echo sequence. Available DICOM header information indicated a repetition time of 130 ms and echo times of 2.38 ms and 4.76 ms. Slice thickness was 6 mm, spacing between slices was 7.2 mm, and pixel spacing was 0.78125 mm  0.78125 mm. The matrix size was 416  512, and the flip angle was . At 1.5 T, the echo at approximately 2.38 ms was treated as the opposed-phase image. The echo at approximately 4.76 ms was treated as the in-phase image.
032		L3 muscle annotation and target definition
033		Manual segmentation masks were created at the L3 level using ITK-SNAP and 3D Slicer. Two physicians participated in the annotation workflow. An assistant physician performed the initial screening and primary annotation. A senior physician then reviewed and corrected the annotated masks. The segmentation target contained four classes: background, abdominal wall muscle, psoas muscle, and paraspinal muscle. These labels were used as the ground truth for model training and evaluation.
034		The L3 center slice was identified for each case. Three adjacent slices centered on the L3 level were used during model training. For evaluation, the center L3 slice was used to match the clinical single-slice L3 body-composition setting. All masks and images were spatially standardized to 256  256 pixels before training.
035		Preprocessing and Dixon-derived weak representation
036		For each case, the in-phase image  and opposed-phase image  were extracted and normalized. A foreground body mask was generated from finite nonzero signal regions. Hole filling and largest-component filtering were used to reduce background artifacts. Two image-derived maps were then computed from the raw in-phase and opposed-phase images:
037		D = I_in - I_opp	(1)
038		C = (I_in - I_opp)/(I_in + I_opp + epsilon)	(2)
039		where  was set to  for numerical stability. The  map emphasizes absolute signal differences between the in-phase and opposed-phase images. The  map normalizes this difference by the local signal magnitude and reduces sensitivity to intensity scale. Both maps were computed inside the body mask, clipped by case-wise percentiles, and z-score normalized. Background voxels outside the body mask were set to zero.
040		The derived  and  maps were used only as weak supervision targets for the representation branch. They were not used as direct segmentation inputs. This distinction is important because these maps were heuristic in-/opposed-phase-derived representations. They were not vendor-generated water-fat separation maps, proton-density fat-fraction maps, or quantitative Dixon outputs.
041		Network architecture and training objective
042		The proposed model used a weakly supervised representation branch followed by a 2D U-Net segmentation backbone (Fig. 2). The raw model input was the two-channel image pair . The representation branch predicted a three-channel learned representation:
043		P = f_rep([I_in, I_opp]) = [P_D, P_C, P_free]	(3)
044		where  and  were weakly supervised by the derived  and  maps. The free channel  was optimized only through the downstream segmentation loss. The representation branch consisted of two 3  3 convolutional blocks with instance normalization and LeakyReLU activation. These blocks were followed by a 1  1 convolution and Tanh activation. The learned representation was concatenated with the original in-phase and opposed-phase images to form a five-channel segmentation input:
045		Z = [I_in, I_opp, P_D, P_C, P_free]	(4)
046		The U-Net segmentation backbone used a base channel width of 32 and four resolution levels. It used instance normalization, LeakyReLU activation, and bilinear upsampling.

051		The segmentation objective combined cross-entropy loss and foreground Dice loss. The Dice term was computed over the three muscle classes and excluded the background class. The representation loss was defined as the  loss between the supervised representation channels and their corresponding weak targets:
052		L_rep = L_1(P_D, D) + L_1(P_C, C)	(5)
053		The total training loss was
054		L_total = L_seg + 0.05 L_rep	(6)
055		This weighting provided Dixon-derived guidance to the representation branch without forcing the final segmentation to depend directly on the handcrafted maps.
056		Internal cross-validation and external testing protocol
057		The internal cohort of 150 cases was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation. In each fold, 30 cases were held out as the internal test fold. The remaining 120 cases were split into 105 training cases and 15 validation cases. This corresponded to 70% training, 10% validation, and 20% internal testing. The validation set was used for model selection and learning-rate scheduling. The held-out internal test fold was used only for internal testing. The independent external cohort of 55 cases was evaluated after model development and was not used for training, validation, or checkpoint selection.
058		Training used all three L3-centered slices from each case, whereas final evaluation used the center L3 slice. Data augmentation included random rotation, scaling, translation, horizontal flipping, Gaussian noise, brightness and contrast adjustment, and gamma augmentation. Spatial augmentations were applied consistently to images, weak representation targets, and masks. Intensity augmentations were applied only to the in-phase and opposed-phase source images. Models were trained for up to 200 epochs using AdamW. The initial learning rate was , and weight decay was . A ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler monitored validation mean foreground Dice. Early stopping was applied with a patience of 30 epochs. The checkpoint with the best validation mean foreground Dice was used for testing.
059		Segmentation evaluation metrics
060		Segmentation performance was evaluated using metrics averaged over the three foreground muscle classes. The primary overlap metrics were mean foreground Dice similarity coefficient and mean foreground intersection-over-union (IoU). Boundary accuracy was evaluated using mean foreground 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95) and mean foreground average symmetric surface distance (ASSD). These four metrics were the primary basis for comparing training strategies, backbone variants, official baseline networks, and ablation experiments.
061		SMI computation and statistical analysis
062		For each annotated or predicted L3 mask, skeletal muscle area (SMA) was calculated from the pixel count of each muscle class and the corresponding in-plane pixel spacing. Compartment-specific SMA values were computed for abdominal wall, psoas, and paraspinal muscles. Total SMA was obtained by summing the three compartments. SMI was then calculated as
063		SMI = SMA / height^2	(7)
064		where SMA was expressed in  and height was expressed in meters. This yielded abdominal wall SMI, psoas SMI, paraspinal SMI, and total SMI.
065		The clinical association between manual-mask-derived SMI and nutrition status was assessed in the subset with complete height and nutrition-status labels. Descriptive statistics were reported by nutrition group. Continuous variables were assessed for distributional normality. Group comparisons were then performed using appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests. Logistic regression was used to evaluate associations between SMI measurements and malnutrition status. These analyses included univariable models and multivariable models adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. Multiple testing across SMI compartments was controlled using Holm correction. Agreement between predicted-mask-derived SMI and manual-mask-derived SMI was assessed using absolute error metrics, relative error metrics, Pearson and Spearman correlations, intraclass correlation coefficients, and Bland–Altman analysis.

071		Comparison of Dixon input and representation strategies
072		The main strategy comparison evaluated whether Dixon-derived information performed better as raw multichannel input or as weak supervision for representation learning. The proposed 3B strategy showed the best overall balance across the four primary foreground metrics (Table [tab:main_strategy]). Compared with in-/opposed-phase input alone, 3B increased mean foreground Dice from 0.9077 to 0.9099. It also reduced ASSD from 1.3090 to 1.2785. Directly adding the derived  and  maps as input channels improved performance relative to in-/opposed-phase input alone. However, the weak-representation strategy achieved slightly higher overlap and lower distance errors than direct four-map input.
073		Comparison with alternative backbones and official baselines
074		When the 3B training strategy was applied to different segmentation backbones, the U-Net backbone produced the best or tied-best performance across the primary metrics (Table [tab:backbone_official]). UNet++ achieved a similar Dice score, but U-Net had lower HD95 and ASSD. DeepLabV3+ had the lowest overlap and weakest boundary performance among the evaluated backbones.
075		The proposed method was also compared with official baseline implementations trained under the same all-slice training and center-slice evaluation setting. The proposed 3B model achieved the highest mean foreground Dice and IoU. It also achieved the lowest HD95 and ASSD. The margin over the official Attention U-Net baseline was small for overlap metrics, whereas the distance metrics favored the proposed model more clearly.
076		Ablation study
077		The ablation experiments evaluated the contribution of the task-driven free representation channel and the weak representation supervision loss (Table [tab:ablation]). Removing  reduced the mean foreground Dice from 0.9099 to 0.9083. It also increased ASSD from 1.2785 to 1.3338. Removing the representation loss produced slightly higher Dice and IoU than the full model, but worsened the distance metrics. The full 3B model achieved the best boundary-oriented performance and the most balanced profile across overlap and surface-distance metrics.

095		Discussion
096		This study developed a weakly supervised Dixon-derived representation learning framework for multiclass L3 skeletal muscle segmentation on abdominal MRI. The main finding was that in-phase and opposed-phase Dixon images were useful beyond raw segmentation input. They also provided auxiliary image-derived supervision that improved the balance between overlap accuracy and boundary accuracy. The proposed 3B strategy achieved stable foreground Dice, IoU, HD95, and ASSD across the three muscle compartments. Downstream analysis further showed that the resulting masks could be converted into SMI measurements. Agreement was particularly high for total SMI and abdominal wall SMI. Together, these results support the feasibility of MRI-based L3 muscle quantification when CT-based body-composition analysis is unavailable or undesirable.
097		The performance pattern suggests that the benefit of the method came from how Dixon-specific signal relationships were incorporated. Directly concatenating the derived  and  maps improved performance relative to in-phase and opposed-phase images alone. This finding indicates that the signal difference between the two Dixon acquisitions contains useful segmentation information. However, using  and  as weak supervision targets produced a more favorable trade-off than direct four-channel input. This distinction matters because the derived maps are heuristic representations of in-/opposed-phase signal behavior. They are not quantitative water–fat images or proton-density fat-fraction maps. Weak supervision allowed the model to learn Dixon-guided features while preserving flexibility in the final segmentation network. The ablation results were consistent with this interpretation. Removing either the free representation channel or the representation loss changed the metric balance, whereas the full model provided the most consistent boundary-distance performance.
098		The comparisons with alternative backbones and official baseline networks further indicate that the main contribution was not simply a larger or more complex architecture. U-Net, U-Net++, Attention U-Net, ResUNet, and DeepLabV3+ all produced strong segmentation results. Nevertheless, the proposed 3B configuration with a conventional U-Net backbone maintained the best or most balanced foreground performance. The differences in overlap metrics were modest, which is expected for strong baselines on a well-defined anatomical task. The distance metrics and ablation behavior, however, suggest a more specific advantage. Dixon-derived representation learning may help stabilize boundary localization around muscle–fat interfaces and regions with fat infiltration or low tissue contrast.
099		These findings are relevant to the gap between CT-based and MRI-based L3 body-composition analysis. CT-based L3 muscle segmentation benefits from standardized Hounsfield-unit information, established attenuation ranges, and large automated pipelines (Weston et al. 2019; Ha et al. 2021). MRI offers different advantages, including lack of ionizing radiation and strong soft-tissue contrast. Dixon MRI has also been increasingly used for abdominal and whole-body body-composition assessment (Borga 2018; Huber et al. 2020; Estrada et al. 2019; Küstner et al. 2020; Nowak et al. 2025). However, MRI intensity values are less standardized than CT attenuation values. L3 muscle boundaries can also be influenced by sequence parameters, motion, partial-volume effects, and fat infiltration. The present study therefore focused on an MRI-specific segmentation setting rather than transferring a CT-oriented pipeline directly to MRI.
100		The SMI analyses extend the segmentation task toward clinically interpretable body-composition measurements. Manual-mask-derived total SMI, abdominal wall SMI, and psoas SMI were associated with malnutrition after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI. Paraspinal SMI was not independently associated in this cohort. The stronger association for total SMI and abdominal wall SMI may reflect their larger muscle areas and more stable measurement behavior. Predicted-mask-derived total SMI and abdominal wall SMI also showed excellent agreement with manual-mask-derived values. This supports their potential use in downstream nutritional and body-composition analyses. In contrast, psoas SMI showed lower agreement. Small compartment size and compartment-assignment errors may therefore have a larger relative effect on this measurement. Total SMI and abdominal wall SMI appear to be the most robust MRI-derived indices in the current workflow. Psoas- and paraspinal-specific analyses should be interpreted more cautiously.
101		Several limitations should be considered. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study. The MRI data came from a specific institutional acquisition setting with Siemens 1.5-T abdominal T1 Dixon imaging. Although an independent cohort was included for testing, broader validation remains necessary. Future validation should include multiple institutions, scanner vendors, field strengths, and Dixon protocols before clinical deployment. Second, the representation targets  and  were derived from in-phase and opposed-phase signal differences. They should not be interpreted as quantitative fat-fraction or vendor-reconstructed water–fat images. Third, the SMI association analysis used only cases with complete height and nutrition-status labels. Nutrition status was assigned by clinical nutrition-department assessment rather than by a prospective standardized trial protocol. Fourth, privacy, ethics, and institutional data-governance restrictions limit public release of clinical imaging data and trained weights. Code and aggregate statistical results may be shared in restricted or supplementary form where permitted.
102		Future work should focus on external reproducibility and clinical translation. Multicenter validation with different scanners and MRI protocols will be needed to assess generalization beyond the current acquisition setting. The workflow could also be extended with automatic L3 level identification, uncertainty estimation, and quality-control flags. These additions would be useful for cases with severe artifacts, atypical anatomy, or ambiguous muscle boundaries. Finally, predicted-mask-derived SMI should be evaluated in larger nutrition-related models and prospective clinical cohorts. Such work would clarify whether automated MRI-derived L3 muscle measurements can serve as reliable quantitative biomarkers in MRI-only patient cohorts.
103		Conclusion
104		This study proposed a weakly supervised Dixon-derived representation learning framework for automated multiclass L3 skeletal muscle segmentation on abdominal MRI. The method used in-phase and opposed-phase Dixon images to guide intermediate representation learning, rather than simply adding handcrafted Dixon-derived maps as direct inputs.
105		The proposed framework achieved stable segmentation performance across abdominal wall, psoas, and paraspinal muscles. It also showed favorable boundary-distance behavior in comparison with direct input strategies, alternative backbones, official baselines, and ablated variants. The resulting masks supported MRI-derived SMI estimation, with strong agreement for total SMI and abdominal wall SMI. Manual-mask-derived SMI also showed nutrition-related associations in the clinical analysis subset.
106		These findings support Dixon MRI-based L3 muscle segmentation as a quantitative tool for body-composition research in MRI-only patient cohorts. Broader multicenter validation remains necessary before routine clinical use.
